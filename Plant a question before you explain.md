# Plant a question before you explain

🌱 **Seed** — distilled from one article and my own take; not yet tested in my talks.

Clarity isn't enough. The brain protects the mental models it already has, and only takes in new information when it *wants* the answer. So the moment before the explanation deserves as much design attention as the explanation itself: **plant a question in the learner's mind, ideally one they start answering with a prediction, before you give them the information.**

Compare:

- Announce: "I'm going to teach you how the stomach replaces its lining every three or four days."
- Plant: "The acid in your stomach can eat through metal. How does your stomach survive?"

The second never needs to ask for attention. People are already guessing, and the answer arrives into a gap they've opened themselves, so they care whether they were right.

## The move

Don't tell people what you're about to teach. Give them something that makes them predict, then let the content resolve it. The prediction can be wrong, and that's useful: a wrong guess marks exactly where their model needs updating.

Four ways to plant it (from the article):

- **Puzzle:** a fact framed as a mystery (the stomach acid).
- **Contradiction:** "You ate 2 slices of pizza, your friend ate 3, but you ate more pizza. How?" (fractions).
- **Prediction:** "Every honeybee in your garden vanishes overnight. What stops working first?" (ecosystems).
- **Dilemma:** "A sea wall protects your beach but speeds erosion down the coast. Whose beach do you protect?" (erosion).

## Why it works (as the article tells it)

- **Curiosity is a gap.** Loewenstein: curiosity arises when we notice a gap between what we know and what we want to know. No gap, no pull.
- **Curiosity helps memory.** Curious states recruit reward circuitry and improve hippocampus-dependent learning, including for incidental material (Kang 2009, Gruber 2014).
- **Conceptual change needs dissatisfaction.** People revise a model when the old one visibly fails and a new one looks intelligible and plausible (Posner 1982; also Vosniadou 1994, Chi 2008).
- **Not an argument for "no guidance".** The questions come first and the explanation still follows. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) and cognitive load theory (Sweller 1988) warn that unguided discovery overloads novices.

## Where I want to use it

- My talks and lectures (e.g. the Alexander talk): open each section with a question or a surprising contradiction instead of a topic heading. Ask the room to guess before I reveal.
- [[Second Renaissance]] material: replace "here is the framework" with the puzzle the framework answers.
- Writing generally: the first line should open a gap, not announce the subject. Works for a post, a thread or a slide.
