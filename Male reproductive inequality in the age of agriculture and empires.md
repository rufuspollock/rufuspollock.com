# Male reproductive inequality in the age of agriculture and empires

This is a map of content on the evidence — genetic, historical, and archaeological — for a massive, long-running inequality in male reproductive access that emerged with the rise of agriculture and states, and peaked in the age of empires.

The core finding: somewhere between roughly 10,000 and 2,000–3,000 years ago (8,000 BCE down to about 1,000 BCE, the Bronze Age and age of empires), a small number of dominant males came to control disproportionate reproductive access to women, while the bulk of males left little or no genetic legacy. The Y-chromosome record preserves this as a striking bottleneck.

## Key entries

- [[A recent bottleneck of Y chromosome diversity coincides with a global change in culture]] — Karmin et al. (2015), Genome Research. The central genetic evidence: a sharp bottleneck in Y-chromosome diversity within the last 10,000 years, with female Ne up to 17× male Ne around 8–4 kya, attributed to cultural changes in male reproductive variance (possibly compounded by conquest, expansion of farming, and transport innovations). The Mongol expansions are cited as a historical example of the same phenomenon.

## Further reading

- The Genghis Khan lineage story ( Zerjal et al. 2003, "The genetic legacy of the Mongols", Am J Hum Genet) — the modern, named instance of the same pattern: one man's Y-chromosomal lineage swamping a huge region. Referenced in the Karmin paper.
